# Who Is Still Stepping on Snow: Hemu Village in Deep-Snow Season

6 AM in Hemu Village, minus 18°C.

I stepped out of the wooden door of the guesthouse wearing two pairs of pants, three pairs of socks, snow gaiters, a down jacket, and mittens. The first step into the snow came up to my ankles. The second step I was a little more careful, following the line of boot prints left by whoever came before. By the third and fourth step, my legs started to disobey me.

But the moment I looked up — it was all worth it.

## The Fairy-Tale Village

Hemu Village is at the **northernmost tip of Burqin County, Xinjiang**, right on the China-Russia-Kazakhstan-Mongolia border. The whole village spreads along the Hemu River valley — all **Tuvan wooden cabins** built of logs, steep-pitched timber roofs, wood smoked dark brown by the chimney smoke. A few houses at the edge of the village have just been renewed: the logs are pale and newly cut, and the gables and ridges are sheathed in dark grey metal that catches what little light there is on an overcast day. The village has over 1,400 households of Tuvans and Kazakh herders who have lived here for generations, almost cut off from the world during the deep-snow winter.

I was there April 15-17 — **the tail end of deep-snow season**. The snow falls from November and doesn't melt until late April — a full six months. Seen from above, the whole settlement looks dusted with sugar: wooden roofs, field ridges, tree tops all white, and the river between the village and the conifers reduced to a single bend. Step inside the village, though, and you realize the snow has already retreated to the shaded corners — the grass in front of the houses shows through, a stretch of dead yellow with a few unmelted piles still lying on it; the fences are still standing, and a plank walkway runs right up to the gate.

In the early morning, the cooking smoke rises from the chimneys, going straight up ten-plus meters in the cold air, then dispersing pale blue when the wind catches it. What the pictures actually preserve is a quieter version of the same thing: **a white snow plain, roofs weighed down with snow, birches and spruces beaten white with frost, and the last of the snow on the far ridges**. When Chinese National Geographic picked the "Ten Most Beautiful Snow Villages of China," Hemu is always at the top of the list.

## People Who Live in the Snow

At 7 AM, carriage driver **Dawuleti** was already busy in his stable. He had to bring his horses to the Hemu Bridge at the village entrance by 8 AM to wait for guests. I asked him if it was cold coming out this early. In his heavy Tuvan accent he said: "Not cold. The horses are cold. Horses don't wear clothes."

His daily work was ferrying guests from the village entrance to the observation deck behind the village — a 1.5 km one-way trip. The driver has to stand on the horse-drawn sled, controlling direction with reins and shouts. The horses' hooves sink into the snow up to their bellies, but they walk steadily.

When we reached the observation deck, the light was still flat and grey. **The whole Hemu village lay quiet on the snow plain**, the brown of the cabins and the white of the snow merging into one another. Nobody spoke; there was only the sound of shutters clicking.

Every morning here was overcast, and the village and the snow plain came out in an even grey-blue. The quieter instants are the ones that stayed — the village from above, buried under snow like a dusting of sugar; the far shore of the lake reduced to a single white line; and a set of boot prints in a forest track that belonged to no one but me.

## Who Is Still Stepping on Snow

On my last afternoon in Hemu, I walked alone into the birch forest outside the village.

The forest is not dense. Birches stand in clumps on the gentle slope, spruces wedged in among them, every branch tip loaded with frost, and underfoot a sheet of snow polished to a shine. You can see the whole grey-white sky overhead — that painted wooden fence with the half-open wood gate stands right at the edge of the trees.

On the ground lies a thick layer of fresh snow, so soft it can barely hold a person up. I stepped down and my whole boot sank in; when I lifted it, the snow slowly closed over again, leaving a shallow pit with ragged edges. **The sound was muffled, like someone sighing under the snow**, and it didn't carry far.

I walked for about twenty minutes, leaving a lonely trail of footprints behind me. Besides me, I couldn't see anyone in the whole forest. Now and then the surface showed a scatter of small round holes — whose, I couldn't say.

In that moment I suddenly wondered: **In this snowfield, who is still stepping?**

It's Dawuleti rushing the horse carriage, it's the old Tuvan granny carrying firewood on her back, it's whatever small things forage under the snow, it's the grass roots, it's winter itself. After the snow is stepped on it leaves marks, but the next snowfall smooths everything over. In Hemu, the act of "stepping" is actually meaningless — every step you take will be covered by new snow tomorrow.

> Do we go to Hemu to see the snow? Maybe. But what I'll remember is that quiet of "tomorrow everything will be gone."
