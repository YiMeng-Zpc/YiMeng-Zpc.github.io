# Tbilisi, Georgia: A Dialogue Between Old and New

The flight from Yerevan to Tbilisi took only 40 minutes, but stepping off the plane you know it's a different country — signs change to Georgia's unique **Mkhedruli script**, taxi drivers speak a different language you can't understand at all, and even the supermarket yogurt packaging has a different name.

Tbilisi is the capital of Georgia. Its very name means "warm" — **tbili** — because of the **sulfur hot springs** that have been steaming here since the 5th century.

## Old Town: Hills, Balconies, Churches

**Narikala Fortress** squats on the hilltop on the west bank of the Kura River, dating back to the 4th century, though what you see today is mostly 17th-century walls. The stone gate and crenellated tower here are part of it. Climb from the old town up narrow alleys and you'll reach the summit in half an hour. Next to the walls stands the white **Mother Georgia** (Kartlis Deda) statue, erected in 2002 — one hand holds a wine cup, the other a sword, symbolizing this nation's tradition of both hospitality and courage.

The old town's houses crowd onto the slopes on both sides of the Kura. The typical Tbilisi house looks like this: **red tile roofs**, pale grey plaster, wooden-framed windows, and whole hillsides of roofs stacked on top of one another like red scales. The narrow lanes burrow downhill with buildings on either side nearly touching. What the old town is really famous for are the **cantilevered wooden balconies** — the deep-carved railings and decorated cornices that push the second and even third floor out over the street, some painted sky blue, some ochre, hanging between grey stone walls like strings of ice cream.

Two churches in the old town are worth stopping at. One is a large **Orthodox cathedral**, built of brick and courses of cut stone, with a conical copper roof over the drum and three receding arches down the front, wooden scaffolding still up in the porch. The other is a small **white glazed-tile chapel** halfway up the hill — octagonal, banded with green-and-white tiles, capped by a pointed metal roof, with a tidy flower garden and cypress trees around it. Standing beside it, the whole Kura valley plain runs all the way out to the eastern horizon.

## The Sulfur Baths

Abanotubani (the sulfur bath district) is in the very center of the old town, where the Kura River bends. Round brick domes puff white steam, and the air smells faintly of rotten eggs — that's the **natural sulfur water from underground**.

There are public baths and private rooms (priced at 20-50 GEL per hour). After soaking, you sit on the cool terrace with tea, and the old town of Tbilisi unfolds below you at dusk.

## New Town: Glass, Metro, Modern Georgia

Cross the **Bridge of Peace** (2012) over the Kura and you're in the new town. This steel-and-glass curved bridge lights up at night like the glowing skeleton of a whale.

**Freedom Square** is the new town's heart — surrounded by Parliament, City Hall, the Marriott Hotel — Georgian sandstone facades wrapped around modern steel-and-glass interiors, an architectural treatment that manages to be both respectful and contemporary. Seen from above, it forms the axis that ends the old town: broad pale marble steps descending in tiers, symmetric flowerbeds and pools on either side, the avenue running straight to the gate of Narikala, and the television tower standing far off on the ridge. When the sun is out right, people throw long shadows across the square.

The metro is another surprise. Tbilisi has only two metro lines, but each station is like a small palace — walls of colored marble and enamel murals, some of the most romantic Soviet-era craftsmanship I've seen. Once you've been down there, you don't easily forget it.

## A City That Never Sleeps

Tbilisi has the **longest nightlife** of any city I've visited. At 11 PM the Mtatsminda funicular is still running; at 1 AM the bars outside Fabrika (a Soviet textile mill converted into a creative district) are still queuing up; at 3 AM the fish sellers under the bridge have already started the new day's work. Those daylight hours: the old town up on the hill glowing grey-white under the cloud, the valley plain lit bright by the sun, and a crowd of people holding up their phones around the hand-built clock tower on the square.

It folds 1,500 years into glass-steel bridges, sulfur steam, colorful balconies, and a hilltop Mother Georgia. The morning I left at 4 AM I stood on the hotel balcony for a while; the old town across the river was dawn-painted pale violet. I think I'll be back. Next time I want to climb up to Narikala before sunset, with the statue and the whole city below her in one frame.
