# Japan: Kyoto, Osaka, and Shopping Streets Packed with People

Japan is the kind of country you want to revisit no matter how many times you've been.

## Kyoto

Kyoto is the cultural heart of Japan. Even after centuries of modernization, it still holds over a thousand temples and shrines. What you get here is a different Kyoto. Black pines in a temple garden, clipped into flat clouds; a bronze bell hanging under the eaves; a vermillion five-story pagoda stepping out from behind the pine branches, tier by tier; and another pagoda in dark brown timber standing above the tiled roofs with the ridge line pressing low behind it. There is also an old lane running down a gentle slope, tiled roofs and hand-painted signboards on either side, people streaming downhill, and the far mountains cutting across the end of the street.

The best seasons are cherry blossom in April and autumn foliage in November. Avoid Golden Week (April 29–May 5) and Obon (mid-August).

## Osaka

Tokyo didn't make it into this trip; Osaka did. The Ebisu Bridge spans the Dotonbori canal, always crowded, with stacked layers of signboards on both sides and a banner strung across the bridge itself; the grid-fronted shops along the canal are packed shoulder to shoulder.

Deeper into the shopping streets, Osaka looks different again — lanes only wide enough for two people to pass, a dense web of overhead cables and rows of black globe lamps, and signboards for optical shops, sportswear and cosmetics running on until they vanish into the distance. Further on, the crowd thickens from a trickle into a flood, and a woman in a red dress walks through the mass of signs.

What really fascinates me about Japan is the rail. Tokyo's subway runs from JR to Keio to the Toei lines, and the three levels of transfer corridors sometimes reveal entire underground cities. Japanese trains run to a punctuality measured in seconds — the arrival time is more accurate than your watch.

## On the Road

I missed the Shinkansen this trip. The Japan Rail Pass makes travel simple: the Shinkansen takes 2 hours 15 minutes from Tokyo to Kyoto, 2 hours 30 to Osaka; and at Kyoto Station there are hundreds of ekiben (station bento) to choose from, so take your time picking one. What comes after you step off the train: the covered shopping arcade, a hillside street packed with people, pine shadows in a temple garden, and two pagodas.

Japan is the kind of country you don't need a guide for — convenience-store rice balls, vending-machine drinks, late-night ramen shops, always there.
